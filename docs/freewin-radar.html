Why this works (the actual science, short version)

1. Testing beats re-reading. Reading the cram sheet again feels productive but barely sticks; being forced to retrieve the answer is what builds memory (the “testing effect” — Rowland 2014 meta-analysis of 61 studies; Karpicke 2025). That's why this is a quiz, not a document. Missing a card and then seeing the answer is the mechanism working — expect to miss most cards on round 1. That's not you being dumb, that's the method loading.

2. Say it out loud before flipping. A vague “yeah I kinda know this” passes by familiarity and learns nothing. Producing the actual words (“continual learning → copy old rows + replay”) is the retrieval that counts. Grade yourself honestly — the ❌ button is your friend; missed cards come back until you get them.

3. Space the rounds, even inside 2 days. One massive session decays fast; the same total time split into rounds with hours between them retains dramatically more (Cepeda 2008; Kornell 2009 — even 1–2 spaced cycles inside 3 days beat pure cramming by ~30–40 points a week later). Sleep between rounds does consolidation for free.

4. Your real exam task is EASIER than this drill. In the contest you only need to recognize the trigger words sitting in the statement — recognition is much easier than the free recall you're practicing here. Train at recall level, perform at recognition level: overtrained on purpose.

5. The hooks are silly on purpose. Vivid, weird images (grandpa at dinner, Hungarian weddings) are dual-coded — stored as both words and a picture — which is why they surface under exam stress when dry facts don't (Paivio's dual-coding theory; keyword-mnemonic literature).
